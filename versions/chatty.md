# **2\. Consolidated arguments against the current proposal**

## **2.1 Overarching strategic objection**

The central objection developed throughout our discussions is that Britain faces three interconnected strategic infrastructure challenges:

1. Food security and agricultural resilience.  
2. Energy security and electricity-system resilience.  
3. Digital sovereignty and computing infrastructure.

These should be addressed together rather than allowing development in one sector to undermine the others.

The objection is not opposition to renewable energy, artificial intelligence or data centres in principle.

It is opposition to accepting the proposed scale, location, land-use configuration and concentration of computing infrastructure without a transparent assessment of alternatives and demonstrable public benefit.

The principal question is:

**Why should Britain unnecessarily sacrifice productive agricultural resources to achieve its computing infrastructure objectives if technically and commercially viable alternatives exist?**

This is a proposed strategic position, not an established finding that Longmarsh has failed a legal or engineering test.

## **2.2 Agricultural land, soil and national food security**

### **2.2.1 Loss of productive agricultural capacity**

The development covers approximately 4,700 acres of land across its combined infrastructure areas.

The applicant should establish:

* The agricultural quality of every affected parcel.  
* The proportion classified as Grade 1, Grade 2 and Grade 3a.  
* The proportion classified as Grade 3b, Grade 4 and Grade 5\.  
* Existing agricultural output.  
* Historic crop rotations.  
* Potential future agricultural output.  
* The number of affected agricultural businesses.  
* Impacts on tenants, employees and agricultural contractors.  
* Effects on local processing, storage and distribution businesses.

Best and Most Versatile agricultural land comprises Grades 1, 2 and 3a under the Agricultural Land Classification system.

It has not been established that all 4,700 acres fall within those classifications.

A credible objection should therefore demand parcel-level classification rather than claim that the entire site is the highest-quality agricultural land.

### **2.2.2 Food security must be assessed beyond acreage**

The assessment should not merely count hectares removed from conventional agriculture.

It should evaluate:

* Tonnes of food currently produced.  
* Crop-specific production capacity.  
* Changes to regional production and crop diversity.  
* The role of affected farms in wider agricultural supply chains.  
* Potential consequences for domestic food availability.  
* Whether displaced agricultural production would move elsewhere.  
* Whether displacement could increase reliance on imports.  
* The opportunity cost of dedicating productive land to infrastructure.

The existence and magnitude of these effects require evidence. No specific tonnage of displaced food production has yet been established.

### **2.2.3 Agricultural co-location must be commercially meaningful**

Solar generation can coexist with certain agricultural activities.

However, the retention of grassland, occasional grazing or vegetation beneath and around panels should not automatically be treated as equivalent to retaining existing agricultural production.

Where agrivoltaics or grazing are proposed, the developer should demonstrate:

* The agricultural enterprise involved.  
* Expected commercial output.  
* Agricultural revenue.  
* Practical machinery access.  
* Soil management requirements.  
* Long-term viability.  
* Comparison with the existing agricultural baseline.

The argument is not that agricultural activity beneath solar panels is impossible. It is that any claim of agricultural retention should be quantified.

### **2.2.4 Soil is strategic infrastructure**

Soil should be considered a long-term productive national asset.

Relevant risks requiring assessment include:

* Soil compaction from construction machinery.  
* Disturbance of soil horizons.  
* Loss of soil structure.  
* Changes to organic carbon.  
* Effects on soil biological activity.  
* Changes in soil fertility.  
* Erosion.  
* Altered surface runoff.  
* Damage to agricultural drainage.  
* Contamination.  
* Impacts arising from cable installation.  
* Restoration difficulties after prolonged infrastructure occupation.

The developer should produce an independently monitored baseline soil survey and soil management plan.

### **2.2.5 Agricultural restoration after 60 years**

Our earlier discussions identified a proposed solar operational period of approximately 60 years.

That duration spans multiple farming generations.

Even where infrastructure is ultimately removed, the intervening loss of particular agricultural production remains a material consideration.

The developer should distinguish between:

* Land temporarily unavailable during construction.  
* Land unavailable for conventional cultivation during operation.  
* Land remaining agriculturally productive.  
* Land capable of restoration.  
* Land permanently converted to industrial use.

The earlier statement that specified data centre, battery, substation and western transmission infrastructure would remain after solar decommissioning requires confirmation against the primary project documents.

## **2.7 Power demand, renewable generation and electricity-system impact**

### **2.7.1 Installed capacity is not annual energy generation**

The development proposes approximately:

* 1,000 MW of data centre capacity.

### **2.7.2 Illustrative energy balance**

The following model was developed during our earlier discussion.

**CALCULATION — not a verified Longmarsh operational forecast**

Assumptions:

* Installed solar generating capacity: 1,300 MW.  
* Annual solar capacity factor: 11%.  
* Total facility electrical capacity: 1,000 MW.  
* Average electrical load factor: 90%.  
* Hours in a normal year: 8,760.

Annual solar generation:

```
1,300 MW × 8,760 hours × 0.11
= 1,252,680 MWh
= 1.25268 TWh
```

Annual computing facility electricity demand:

```
1,000 MW × 8,760 hours × 0.90
= 7,884,000 MWh
= 7.884 TWh
```

Solar generation relative to annual electricity demand:

```
1.25268 ÷ 7.884 × 100
= approximately 15.89%
```

Difference between annual electricity demand and annual solar generation:

```
7.884 − 1.25268
= 6.63132 TWh
```

Under these assumptions, the solar development would generate electricity equivalent to approximately 16% of annual data centre consumption.

This does not establish that the project has a 6.63 TWh unserved electricity requirement, because additional grid imports and other electricity procurement arrangements may supply that demand.

It establishes the difference between illustrative on-site solar generation and illustrative facility consumption.

### **2.7.3 Annual energy matching is not hourly matching**

Even where annual renewable generation equals annual consumption, hourly generation and demand can differ significantly.

The developer should publish:

* Hourly electricity demand.  
* Hourly solar generation.  
* Seasonal energy profiles.  
* Expected battery operation.  
* Expected imports and exports.  
* Renewable electricity procurement arrangements.  
* Periods of greatest grid dependence.  
* Performance under adverse weather conditions.

An 8,760-hour annual model would provide a considerably stronger evidence base than comparisons of installed generating capacity.

### **2.7.4 Battery storage**

Battery power capacity and battery energy capacity are different measurements.

* MW describes the rate at which a battery can charge or discharge.  
* MWh describes its stored energy capacity.

The developer should disclose:

* Battery power capacity.  
* Battery energy capacity.  
* Maximum discharge duration.  
* Round-trip efficiency.  
* Expected operating cycles.  
* Degradation.  
* Replacement schedules.  
* Operational purpose.  
* Grid services.  
* Fire safety arrangements.  
* End-of-life treatment.

Battery storage can shift electricity consumption and generation across time, but it does not create additional primary energy.

No specific Longmarsh battery duration has been established.

### **Important legal limitation**

The regulations do not automatically oblige the developer to investigate every alternative proposed by an objector.

They require disclosure of the relevant reasonable alternatives studied, together with the reasons for the chosen option and comparative environmental information.

The strongest use of this provision is therefore to:

1. Identify credible alternatives.  
2. Explain why they may achieve comparable objectives.  
3. Explain their potential environmental advantages.  
4. Ask the developer to include them in its assessment.  
5. Scrutinise the adequacy of the alternatives actually considered.

It would be incorrect to claim that failure to adopt a distributed alternative automatically constitutes a breach of the Environmental Impact Assessment Regulations.

## **Executive summary**

Britain faces three interconnected strategic challenges: food security, energy security and digital sovereignty.

These challenges should not be addressed independently, nor should progress in one area unnecessarily compromise another.

I support the development of British computing infrastructure, artificial intelligence capabilities, renewable energy generation and agricultural diversification. However, I have substantial concerns regarding the scale, configuration and agricultural consequences of the proposed Longmarsh Green Energy development.

The central question is not whether Britain needs additional data centres. It is whether this particular infrastructure architecture represents the most sustainable, resilient and economically beneficial means of providing that capacity.

I request that the developer undertake and publish a transparent assessment of alternative infrastructure configurations, including distributed and hybrid computing models, before finalising its development consent application.

## **Q1. Overall project proposals**

### **1\. Strategic infrastructure alternatives**

The proposal combines approximately 1.3 GW of solar generation with a proposed 1 GW data centre campus, battery storage and associated transmission infrastructure.

The scale of the development warrants a rigorous assessment of alternative configurations, rather than treating the proposed location, capacity and centralised architecture as predetermined.

I request comparative assessment of:

* The currently proposed centralised campus.  
* Alternative brownfield and industrial locations.  
* A smaller centralised campus supported by distributed computing infrastructure.  
* Modular computing facilities integrated with existing agricultural and industrial buildings.  
* Renewable generation through agricultural rooftops, existing infrastructure and lower-quality agricultural land.  
* Phased development tied to evidenced computing demand.  
* Locations with established or demonstrable demand for recovered heat.

Each configuration should be assessed against equivalent computing service requirements, rather than simply installed electrical capacity.

Comparative criteria should include land consumption, agricultural displacement, grid reinforcement, water demand, lifecycle emissions, heat utilisation, network connectivity, resilience, employment, economic value and commercial viability.

I recognise that tightly coupled AI training workloads may require centralised infrastructure. However, inference, data hosting, edge computing and certain other workloads may be suitable for geographically distributed facilities.

The assessment should therefore distinguish between computing workloads rather than assuming that all demand requires a single hyperscale campus.

### **2\. National digital sovereignty**

The developer should explain how the proposed development contributes to meaningful British digital sovereignty.

Physical location within the United Kingdom does not, by itself, establish national control over computing infrastructure.

Please clarify:

* The anticipated ownership and operational structure.  
* Intended categories of computing customers.  
* Availability of computing capacity to British businesses, universities, farmers and public institutions.  
* Applicable jurisdiction and operational control.  
* Supply-chain dependencies and infrastructure resilience.  
* Whether any commitments to domestic computing access will be secured.

Any national security justification should be supported by demonstrable commitments rather than geographical location alone.

### **3\. Electricity generation and consumption**

Installed solar generation capacity must not be conflated with continuous electricity availability.

An illustrative calculation using a solar capacity factor of 11% gives approximately 1.25 TWh of annual generation from 1.3 GW of installed solar capacity.

A facility drawing an average of 900 MW would consume approximately 7.88 TWh annually.

These assumptions are illustrative, but demonstrate the importance of publishing a comprehensive energy balance.

I request:

* Clarification of whether the proposed 1 GW capacity represents IT equipment demand or total facility demand.  
* Forecast power usage effectiveness (PUE).  
* An hourly electricity generation and consumption model covering a representative full year.  
* Seasonal generation and demand profiles.  
* Battery power capacity in MW and energy storage capacity in MWh.  
* Storage duration, efficiency and replacement assumptions.  
* Maximum and expected grid imports and exports.  
* Expected renewable electricity matching on an hourly and annual basis.  
* Assessment of grid congestion, reinforcement requirements and impacts on other electricity consumers.

Claims of sustainability should be based on actual anticipated energy flows, rather than installed renewable generation capacity alone.

* Backup generator operation and testing.  
* Combined noise from multiple infrastructure components.  
* Artificial lighting and impacts on rural dark skies.  
* Glint, glare and landscape intrusion.  
* Construction traffic and road safety.

## **Q3. Environmentally led design**

### **6\. Agricultural land and national food security**

The National Policy Statement for Renewable Energy Infrastructure (EN-3, 2025), particularly paragraphs 2.10.20–2.10.25, establishes a preference for suitable previously developed land and lower-quality agricultural land where agricultural development is necessary.

I request publication of a detailed Agricultural Land Classification assessment identifying the extent of Grades 1, 2, 3a, 3b and lower-quality land affected.

This should distinguish between:

* Permanent industrial development.  
* Land occupied by solar generation.  
* Battery and substation infrastructure.  
* Temporary construction areas.  
* Transmission corridors.  
* Habitat mitigation and landscaping.  
* Land remaining available for commercially meaningful food production.

The presence of vegetation or occasional grazing should not automatically be treated as equivalent to maintaining existing food production.

### **7\. Soil protection and agricultural restoration**

Soil is a strategic natural asset developed over generations.

The environmental assessment should address:

* Soil compaction and structural damage.  
* Soil organic carbon.  
* Soil biological activity and fertility.  
* Agricultural drainage.  
* Erosion and runoff.  
* Construction disturbance.  
* Potential contamination.  
* Long-term restoration feasibility.

### **8\. Water resources and cooling**

The developer proposes a sealed closed-loop cooling system.

While this may substantially reduce water consumption, closed-loop cooling describes the circulation of cooling fluid and does not necessarily establish the overall water requirements of a facility.

The method by which heat is ultimately rejected remains important.

I request disclosure of:

* Cooling architecture and final heat rejection method.  
* Initial fill and ongoing replacement water requirements.  
* Annual and peak water consumption.  
* Evaporative or adiabatic cooling, if applicable.  
* Water source and supply arrangements.  
* Operational performance during extreme temperatures.  
* Drought contingency plans.  
* Wastewater and discharge arrangements.  
* Effects on agricultural abstraction and local water security.

The assessment should demonstrate compatibility with regional water availability and future climate conditions.

A data centre consuming 1 GW continuously would convert approximately 8.76 TWh of electrical energy into heat annually, before accounting for utilisation and heat recovery constraints.

Not all of this heat would be technically or economically recoverable. Nevertheless, the magnitude of the resource justifies a comprehensive feasibility assessment.

Potential productive applications include:

* Commercial greenhouse horticulture.  
* Agricultural crop and grain drying.  
* Industrial hemp processing.  
* Controlled-environment food production.  
* Mushroom cultivation.  
* Aquaculture, where appropriate.  
* Food processing.  
* Local industrial and community heating.

The developer should publish a heat utilisation study addressing available temperatures, seasonal demand, heat pump requirements, distribution costs, commercial counterparties and the proportion of energy that could realistically be utilised.

An assessment should also compare the proposed campus with smaller modular facilities located closer to existing agricultural and industrial heat demand.

Waste heat utilisation should be considered during site selection, rather than as an optional addition after the principal infrastructure decisions have been made.

### **10\. Flood risk, drainage and biodiversity**

The development's location requires rigorous assessment of drainage and flood resilience.

The assessment should address individual and cumulative impacts on surrounding agricultural land, watercourses, drainage infrastructure and nearby settlements, including future climate scenarios.

Biodiversity commitments should distinguish between genuine ecological improvements, mitigation of development impacts and the displacement of productive agriculture.

Potential improvements in biodiversity should not be used to obscure unrelated adverse impacts on food production or soil resources.

# **7\. Corrections, contradictions, omissions and unresolved evidence**

This section records material qualifications to our earlier discussions and identifies issues that must not be silently converted into established facts.

## **7.1 The development area is not the data centre footprint**

**Earlier interpretation:** A 1 GW data centre development occupying approximately 4,700 acres.

**Clarification:** The approximately 4,700-acre figure relates to the combined Longmarsh development, including solar generation, battery storage, computing infrastructure and associated components.

The specific permanent data centre footprint has not been established in this report.

## **7.2 Agricultural land quality**

**Earlier discussion:** The development includes high-quality agricultural land.

**Clarification:** The exact distribution of Agricultural Land Classification grades has not been established.

It must not be claimed that all 4,700 acres are Grade 1, Grade 2 or Grade 3a agricultural land.

A detailed survey and mapping exercise is necessary.

## **7.3 Temporary versus permanent development**

**Earlier discussion:** The solar development would operate for 60 years, while significant industrial infrastructure would remain.

**Current verification:** Confirmed by the developer's proposals.

The Western Site data centre, substation, associated battery storage and Western Cable Route are intended to remain following solar decommissioning.

This is one of the strongest project-specific facts established during consolidation.

Source: [https\://longmarshgreenenergy.co.uk/proposals](https://longmarshgreenenergy.co.uk/proposals)

## **7.4 Grid connection and operational dates**

**Earlier discussion:** Grid connection in 2031\.

**Additional finding:** Construction is anticipated from 2029, with the development intended to become operational by 2032\.

The dates describe different project milestones.

Neither is a guaranteed delivery date.

## **7.5 The £25 billion figure**

**Earlier discussion:** A £25 billion development.

**Clarification:** The developer expects the data centre campus to attract investment exceeding £25 billion.

This is an investment projection, not evidence of committed or secured financing.

The distinction should be maintained throughout future documents.

## **7.6 Renewable generation and computing demand**

**Earlier discussion:** A 1.3 GW solar installation accompanying a 1 GW data centre.

**Clarification:** Installed generating capacity is not equivalent to continuous electricity supply.

The approximately 16% solar contribution calculated earlier is based on an assumed 11% solar capacity factor and 90% computing electrical load factor.

These are illustrative engineering assumptions, not figures established for Longmarsh.

The developer also identifies offshore wind generation and grid electricity imports as part of its proposed energy supply context.

## **7.7 Waste heat**

**Earlier discussion:** A continuous 1 GW electrical load produces approximately 8.76 TWh of heat annually.

**Clarification:** This is a theoretical energy conversion calculation.

It does not establish:

* Actual annual Longmarsh electrical consumption.  
* Actual annual thermal output.  
* Recoverable heat.  
* Economically usable heat.  
* Heat demand.  
* Commercial revenue from heat recovery.


## **7.8 Water consumption**

**Earlier discussion:** Concerns over data centre water consumption.

**Clarification:** Longmarsh specifically proposes closed-loop cooling and expects limited water consumption.

It would be incorrect to apply water consumption figures from unrelated evaporatively cooled data centres directly to Longmarsh.

The technical objection should focus on the actual heat rejection architecture and independently quantified water demand.

The original consultation response identifies distances of 30 metres, 100 metres and 300 metres.

The developer's project proposals independently confirm the intention to locate the data centre approximately 300 metres from nearby homes and other noise-sensitive receptors.
